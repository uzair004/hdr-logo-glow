Should our logo glow?

There is a trick that makes an image on LinkedIn shine much brighter than everything else on the page — brighter than the screen's normal white. It only works on newer Apple screens. A few companies have started doing it and their logo jumps out of the feed. We can do it with our logo. This page is to decide whether we should, and how far.

Checking your screen…

Read this first

Two things to know before you look at anything below, or the page will not make sense.

  • Only some screens can show it. A recent MacBook Pro, a Pro Display or an iPhone 12 and newer can go far brighter for small parts of an image. Older laptops, most external monitors and most Windows machines cannot. On those, every picture below looks completely ordinary — and that is not a bug, that is the honest answer for those people.
  • You cannot screenshot it. A screenshot flattens it back to normal white. If you paste this into Slack to ask a colleague, they will see nothing. Send them this link instead.

The choice

There are two ways to do this to our logo, and they are opposites. Our logo is a white shape on a black square. Either the shape glows, or the square glows.

Each card below shows the same option twice: on the left, how it looks to someone with a bright Apple screen; on the right, how it looks to everyone else. That second column is the part that matters most, because most people are in it.

Option 1

The shape glows, the square stays black

The white shape lights up like a neon sign. The black around it stays properly black — on these screens the backlight behind black actually switches off, so you get a very sharp contrast in a small space.

On an ordinary screen it is exactly our logo as it is today. Nobody sees a change. This is the safe version: people with the right screen get something extra, and nobody loses anything.

Option 2

The square glows, the shape goes black

We flip the logo. Now nearly the whole square is lit and our shape reads as a cut-out silhouette. Around nine times more of the square is glowing than in option 1, so it is far more noticeable in a busy feed. This is what Haladir, the company that started this, actually does.

But on an ordinary screen our logo becomes a white square with a black shape. That is a real change to our brand for the majority of people who will never see the glow. It also throws light across the text next to it, which is unpleasant to read at night.

Side by side with the company that did it first, option 2 also reads as a copy rather than as our own idea.

How strong

If we go with option 1, how far do we push it?

The file can ask the screen for any amount of extra brightness. Past a point the screen simply cannot go further, so asking for more stops adding much and starts looking like a mistake rather than a design choice. Four settings, quietest to loudest:

  • 1 · Gentle — you notice it only if you are looking for it. Safe, but probably not worth doing at all.
  • 2 · Noticeable — clearly brighter than the page, still calm.
  • 3 · Strong — the level Haladir and the others use. Unmistakable, still looks deliberate.
  • 4 · Maximum — the screen is being asked for more than it can give. It stops getting brighter and starts looking flat and blown out, and it is the level people complain about.

What it costs

Neither option costs money or engineering time — it is one image upload, and we can undo it by uploading the old file again. The real costs are these:

  • It will annoy some people. A bright patch in a feed at night is genuinely uncomfortable, and worse for anyone with migraine or light sensitivity. Option 1 keeps the bright area small, which is most of why it is the gentler choice. Option 2 lights up the whole square.
  • It may stop working. LinkedIn can switch this off for everyone with a small change to their site, and Slack already has. Treat it as something that runs for a few months, not as our permanent logo.
  • Option 2 changes our brand for most people. Everyone on an ordinary screen would see a white square instead of our black one, permanently, for no benefit to them.
  • Some will find it gimmicky. It is a trick, and a well-known one now. It reads as clever from a startup and as desperate from anyone else.

Suggestion

Option 1, strong

Our logo is already a white shape on a black square, which happens to be the perfect shape for this. The shape lights up, the square stays black, and anyone on an ordinary screen sees the logo we have always had.

Option 2 is louder, but we would be giving up our black logo for the majority of people in exchange for an effect only a minority can see — and standing next to Haladir it looks borrowed.

If we go ahead, one small thing is needed first: the best copy of our logo I could find is a small, slightly compressed image. A clean original file would make the edges noticeably sharper at this brightness.

Why it works

Short version, no background needed. A normal image can only say “this pixel is white”, and the screen decides how bright white is — usually about a fifth of what the screen can actually do. The rest of that capacity is kept in reserve for films and photos.

This trick uses the format that films are stored in, where the image says an exact brightness in real units rather than just “white”. Our logo asks for a brightness far above normal white, the screen obliges, and everything else on LinkedIn stays at its usual fifth. Nothing is glowing — our logo is simply at full power while the whole page around it is turned down.
